# training-phase-selection — choose the next training phase

**Purpose**
Give coaching principles for choosing the next training phase. Choose the priority before the program. Program families differ in emphasis, not in kind.

**Trigger**
A lifter or coach asks what to train next, which program to run next, or how to choose the next training phase.

**Do not use for**
- Medical diagnosis, treatment, or rehab prescription
- Writing a full custom program from a blank page. This skill chooses a phase and a program family, not every set and rep.

## Principles

### 1. Choose the priority before the program

Program families — strength, hypertrophy, powerbuilding, general strength and conditioning, and others — use the same kind of training. They differ in what they emphasize. Do not start by picking a named template. First decide what this phase is for.

### 2. Answer five questions before you choose

- What is the emphasis for this phase?
- What does recent training history suggest?
- What schedule and dose can really be sustained?
- How much specificity does the goal need?
- Which of the good options does the lifter prefer?

A good next phase answers all five. A program that looks ideal on paper but fails one of them is not a good fit.

### 3. Experience level is about the timescale of progress

Experience level means how fast progress can be detected above noise. Newer lifters often show change from session to session or week to week. For experienced lifters, judge progress over weeks or months. Do not treat a few flat sessions as a failed phase.

### 4. The plan must survive real life

Prefer a sustainable dose over a theoretically better schedule that keeps getting missed. A slightly smaller plan that is completed beats a larger plan that is skipped.

### 5. Specificity is not rigidity

The phase can stay specific to the goal without locking every exercise. Substitutions are fine for equipment, anatomy, symptoms, or preference, as long as they still serve the phase emphasis.

### 6. Preference is a legitimate variable

When more than one option fits the priority, history, schedule, and specificity, the lifter's preference is a valid reason to choose. Preference is not a leftover after the "real" criteria. It is one of the criteria.

## How to use this skill

1. Name the emphasis for this phase in one sentence.
2. Answer the five questions with the lifter. Write the answers down.
3. Choose the timescale you will use to judge progress, based on how fast change can be seen above noise.
4. Check that the weekly schedule and dose can be sustained in real life. If they cannot, reduce the dose or pick a different family.
5. Pick a program family that matches the emphasis, then a specific program if one is needed.
6. Allow substitutions that still serve the emphasis.
7. If two options still fit, choose the one the lifter prefers.
